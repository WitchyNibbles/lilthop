# Voice safety research

Evidence for the safety, wording and measurement choices in a free, local-only desktop voice training app for trans people. Sources read on 2026-11-06.

Quoted text credited to a source is copied from that source. Quoted app labels and wording ideas are mine. **Inference** marks my own reasoning. **Secondary** marks anything outside the requested high-trust sources. **Gap** marks a question with no good source, and nothing has been filled in. This is research for an app. It is not medical advice, and no clinician has reviewed it.

## Audience decision, 2026-11-08

The owner chose the audience: trans women and transfeminine people who want a higher, more feminine voice. Pitch raising stays and there is no pitch lowering. §6 below avoided "feminization" because no audience had been chosen (ASHA: "may not represent the goals of transgender women, nonbinary clients, or transfeminine clients"). With the audience chosen:

- The intro says once who the app is for, and the pitch goal names the direction ("Raise your speaking pitch").
- S12 no longer says "lower". S1 is rewritten to say the app has no clinical support behind it and doesn't replace a voice therapist, partly in the owner's own words. Both are marked revised in the ready text below.
- A sources note under the range gives the research numbers from §4, the disagreement on a minimum, and the English-speaker caveat.
- What stays: no "passing" (Smith 2020), no promises, no direction picker. "Feminization" stays out of the UI as a process noun (ASHA: "there is no single definition for voice feminization"); the README may use it. "Feminine" appears in the app only in the intro, as the person's own aim, and in the sources note.

**The suggested range.** Under 175 Hz the range starts 1 semitone above the baseline and goes to 5 above, its top capped at 220 Hz and its lower edge never above 175 Hz. From 175 Hz up it sits around the voice, 2 semitones below to 3 above. It never moves on its own: after a new measure the app shows the new suggestion, and only "Use this range" takes it. Saved ranges are never rewritten.

- 175 Hz is the top of the ambiguous zone: ASHA, "145 Hz to 175 Hz". Davies: a training pitch "that is either in gender neutral or low cisfemale range and can be produced without strain."
- 220 Hz is the top of the Davies 2015 feminine row in §4.
- Not pushing a voice that is already high: UCSF, "does not necessarily result in listener perception"; ASHA, "avoid an overemphasis on SFF".
- No automatic stepping: NIDCD, "Avoid using the extremes of your vocal range".
- **Judgement, no source:** the +1 semitone lift, the +5 top, the −2/+3 range around a high voice, and having no pace of progression at all.

**Exercises.** Sustained tone is now a hum and glides aim at the middle and upper voice (Davies, both in §7). Forward vowels is new and never scored: Carew 2007 via Davies, "a forward tongue carriage and slightly spread lip posture during speech" (n=10), and §5, "a meter that rewards a higher centroid could reward pushing". The pitch goal now plans pitch raising after pitch awareness (Söderpalm via Davies, "without strain or vocal damage").

**New safety text, S15 to S17 in the ready text below.**

- S15, raise gently: Davies, "can be produced without strain"; UCSF, raising pitch by tension "requires continuous muscular effort"; UCSF, "does not necessarily result in listener perception". Shown under pitch raising and on the Safety page.
- S16, changes to watch: NIDCD, "Have you lost your ability to hit some high notes when singing?" and "Does your voice suddenly sound deeper?"; Davies, "a speech-language therapist be consulted". Shown with the stop signs on the Safety page.
- S17, all day counts: UCSF, "even successful patients often complain of a sensation of vocal effort and/or fatigue at the end of the day"; NIDCD, "Take vocal naps". Shown on the Safety page.
- The louder notice now says louder can pull pitch up: Liénard, "rates close to 5 Hz/dB for F0", and §3, "a pitch gain that arrives with a level rise may be pushing".

The Spanish wording, "más aguda y femenina" and "transfemeninas" included, still needs a native speaker's review. No clinician has reviewed any of this.

## The main problem to design around

The sources do not endorse self-guided practice, and the app has to be honest about that.

- Davies, Papp & Antoni 2015: "Self-guided voice and communication change without professional supervision is not recommended. Clients intending to pursue self-guided voice change should be encouraged to, at a minimum, have an initial professional assessment and then to consult with their primary care provider if they develop symptoms of vocal fatigue or negative changes to vocal quality." [Full text](https://zenodo.org/records/895532), [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1080/15532739.2015.1075931)
- WPATH SOC8 ch. 14, on the four ways people change their voice: "The currently existing research evidence does not include self-initiated change, but is focused on the latter three approaches." [SOC8](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9553112/)
- ASHA frames apps as an add-on to care: "Various mobile and web-based apps—in conjunction with voice treatment—may assist with generalization and carryover." [ASHA portal](https://www.asha.org/practice-portal/professional-issues/gender-affirming-voice-and-communication/)
- Risk is real but not certain. Davies 2015: "Modifying habitual pitch, adopting unfamiliar articulatory postures or producing the voice in nonhabitual ways can be fatiguing and potentially damaging for some people." The same paper: "Söderpalm, Larsson, and Almquist (2004) established that MtF transsexuals were able to raise their speaking fundamental frequency without strain or vocal damage."
- **Gap:** I found no study measuring how often self-guided practice causes injury. The warnings are expert opinion and clinical reasoning.
- **Inference:** many people cannot reach a clinician, so the app should say plainly that a professional check is the recommended path, then give the best safety text it can. It should never imply that using the app is equal to care.

## 1. Warning signs and when to see someone

**Stop and rest.** No source gives one agreed "stop list". These are the sourced signs:

- NIDCD self-check: "Has your voice become hoarse or raspy?", "Have you lost your ability to hit some high notes when singing?", "Does your voice suddenly sound deeper?", "Does your throat often feel raw, achy, or strained?", "Has it become an effort to talk?", "Do you find yourself repeatedly clearing your throat?" [NIDCD](https://www.nidcd.nih.gov/health/taking-care-your-voice)
- NIDCD: "Avoid speaking or singing when your voice is hoarse or tired." and "Rest your voice when you are sick."
- ASHA: "increased vocal effort associated with speaking, decreased vocal endurance or onset of fatigue with prolonged voice use, variable vocal quality throughout the day or during speaking, running out of breath quickly, frequent coughing or throat clearing (may worsen with increased voice use), and excessive throat or laryngeal tension/pain/tenderness." [ASHA voice disorders](https://www.asha.org/practice-portal/clinical-topics/voice-disorders)
- Vocal fatigue, Nanjundeswaran 2015: "a global syndrome identified by a series of symptoms during or after speech, including a person's perception of increased vocal effort, laryngeal discomfort, neck or shoulder tension, throat or neck pain, reduced pitch range, loss of vocal flexibility, reduced vocal projection or power, reduced vocal control, voice loss, increase in symptoms across the speaking day, and typically, improvement of symptoms with rest." [J Voice](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jvoice.2014.09.012)
- NHS patient handout (**Secondary**, hospital speech therapy service): "None of these exercises should feel painful or uncomfortable, so should this occur, stop the exercises until you have discussed this with your speech and language therapist." [Royal Berkshire NHS](https://www.royalberkshire.nhs.uk/media/5ehceiw2/voice-warm-up-and-warm-down_apr24.pdf)
- **Gap:** "throat tickle" has no direct source. Nearest are NIDCD's "Your throat might feel scratchy" and the throat clearing lines above.

**New is not the same as hurt.** Davies 2015: "Change to any motor habit will feel unfamiliar and possibly uncomfortable simply because it is nonhabitual." This sits awkwardly with the NHS line above. **Inference:** Davies means unfamiliar, the NHS handout means physical discomfort, so app text should separate "feels strange" from "hurts".

**When to see someone.** The numbers differ:

- No waiting period, NIDCD: "You should also see a doctor if you are coughing up blood or if you have difficulty swallowing, feel a lump in your neck, experience pain when speaking or swallowing, have difficulty breathing, or lose your voice completely for more than a few days." [NIDCD](https://www.nidcd.nih.gov/health/hoarseness)
- Sudden loss, NIDCD Spanish page: "Si usted de pronto pierde la voz durante un uso intenso de la voz (como al gritar), es posible que tenga una hemorragia de las cuerdas vocales." and "deben ser tratadas inmediatamente con el reposo total de la voz y una consulta con el médico." - **Inference:** use NIDCD's three weeks as the outer limit because it is the public-facing and shortest single figure, and put Davies' earlier advice first.

## 2. How long and how often

**Gap:** I found no sourced session length or daily limit for beginners practising alone. Davies 2015 gives no minutes at all and leaves it to the clinician: "the speech-language therapist should communicate clearly about what is involved in the therapy program, including length of practice time, the nature of the exercises, and frequency of practice sessions." For vocal function exercises, Bane et al.: "little is known about the ideal dosage, or the dosage that yields the greatest benefit without causing damage (Roy, 2012)." [Bane](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6207485/)

Numbers that do exist, none of them a safety limit:

**Warm-up and cool-down.** Evidence is thin and mostly from singers. Ragsdale 2020, 9 singers: "immediate self-perceived benefit for all participants" after 5 and 10 minutes, but "No significant differences were found in delayed perceptual analyses". [J Voice](<https://www.jvoice.org/article/S0892-1997(20)30297-6/abstract>) Ribeiro: "for the vocal cool-down the nasal sounds, fricative sounds and vibrant of tongue or lips, in descending scales, were the most used exercises."

**Rest.**

- NIDCD: "Take vocal naps—rest your voice throughout the day." UCSF, on the rest of the day counting too: "even successful patients often complain of a sensation of vocal effort and/or fatigue at the end of the day."
- Recovery data come from heavy loading, not gentle practice. Hunter & Titze 2009 (2 hours of loud reading) contradicts itself: the abstract says "90% recovery within 4-6 hours and full recovery at 12-18 hours", the body says "Fifty percent recovery occurred within 4-6 hours; 90 percent recovery occurred within 12-18 hours." [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3311979/) Lei 2020 cites the body version. [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7055723/) Do not quote the abstract figure.
- Shembel & Nanjundeswaran 2022: "clinical signs of acute vocal fold edema that lasts about 24 hours and then resolves with vocal rest and recovery after 72 hours." [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9943805/)
- **Gap:** no source says how many days to rest after a symptom appears during practice.

**Inference for the app:** a default around 15 minutes is defensible as a reminder, borrowed from one supervised program. Call it a pause reminder and never a "safe limit". Do not build streaks or daily quotas. One qualitative study found "Even strong feelings of self-blame and shame emerged from the interviews. People mentioned that it was their own fault and that they did not practice enough." [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9690106/)

## 3. Can a mic or app detect strain?

No, not in any way I could validate. Lab work shows some signal, which is different from a working detector.

- By consensus definition, effort is felt by the speaker. Hunter 2020: "The “vocal effort” is defined as a perceptual phenomenon (as opposed to a physiological phenomenon) experienced by the speaker and not the listener" [JSLHR](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7210446/)
- Scoping review, Wu, Cantor-Cutiva & Hunter 2025: "no standardized acoustic definition of strained voice quality has been established." and "While no single metric reliably captures its full scope, acoustic measures—particularly spectral and cepstral features—can complement perceptual assessments." [J Voice](<https://www.jvoice.org/article/S0892-1997(25)00337-6/abstract>) Nguyen & Madill 2023: "acoustic correlates of perceptions of strained voice have been notoriously difficult to establish." [J Voice](<https://www.jvoice.org/article/S0892-1997(23)00088-7/fulltext>) McKenna & Stepp 2018: "there seems to be no single acoustical measure predictive of perceptual ratings of vocal effort (speaker or listener)". Their model did find SPL, L/H ratio and HNR predicted effort, but that was 26 healthy adults deliberately raising effort. [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6167228/)
- Within-speaker tracking looks better than cross-speaker. Lien 2015: "Relationships between RFF and both the aerodynamic and perceptual measures of vocal effort were weak across speakers (R 2 = .06–.26). Within speakers, relationships were variable but much stronger on average (R 2 = .45–.56)." [JSLHR](https://pubs.asha.org/doi/10.1044/2015_JSLHR-S-14-0194)
- ASHA expert panel: acoustic measures "can provide indirect inferences regarding the underlying pathophysiology of voice disorders". [Patel 2018](https://pubs.asha.org/doi/full/10.1044/2018_AJSLP-17-0009) Hardware matters too. van der Woerd 2020: "Both microphone and recording setting resulted in significant differences across several acoustic measurements." [JSLHR](https://pubs.asha.org/doi/abs/10.1044/2020_JSLHR-20-00212)
- ASHA warns tools can cause harm when used alone: clinicians can teach clients to use them "to avoid inappropriate practice, which can contradict treatment efforts and lead to the development of hyperfunctional voicing patterns." [ASHA portal](https://www.asha.org/practice-portal/professional-issues/gender-affirming-voice-and-communication/)

**Loudness.** **Gap:** I found no sourced numeric loudness limit. Guidance is qualitative: ASHA "talking at a moderate volume"; NIDCD "Talking too loudly and too softly can both stress your voice." A desktop app also cannot report true loudness without calibration. Patel 2018: "(SPL values produced by computer software programs are relative and not absolute measures). Thus, the entire recording system must be calibrated to enable measurement of absolute SPL values." and "The level of the recorded signal is affected by the distance of the microphone from the mouth (must be held constant) and recording system gain/amplification". Švec & Granqvist 2018: "SPL of voice and speech should be reported together with the mouth-to-microphone distance so that the levels can be related to vocal power." [JSLHR](https://pubs.asha.org/doi/10.1044/2017_JSLHR-S-17-0095)

**What the data can honestly say** (**Inference**): pitch tracks your speaking pitch, with its spread over time. Level shows change relative to earlier in the same session on the same mic and distance. Neither can say "safe", "strained", "healthy", "tired" or "damaged". Louder speech also pulls pitch upward, at "rates close to 5 Hz/dB for F0" (Liénard & Di Benedetto 1999, [JASA](http://acts.ing.uniroma1.it/Papers/J12-Lienard_al-JASA99.pdf)), so a pitch gain that arrives with a level rise may be pushing. A hint like "you got louder, try easing off" is honest. "No strain detected" is not, and silence from the app must never read as an all-clear.

No agreed floor. Davies 2015: "there is no consensus in the literature as to how high the speaking fundamental frequency (SFF) should be" and "Studies have suggested a minimum SFF of 155 Hz (Wolfe et al., 1990), 165 Hz (Oates & Dacakis, 1983), 172 Hz (Owen & Hancock, 2010), and 180 Hz (Gorham-Rowan & Morris, 2006)." ASHA, right after its figures: "Practitioners are encouraged to avoid an overemphasis on SFF and to focus on client-specific concerns".

**Pitch alone is not enough.**

- UCSF: "increasing pitch into the female range does not necessarily result in listener perception of the speaker as female."
- Leung 2018 meta-analysis: "speaking fundamental frequency contributing to 41.6% of the variance in gender perception. Auditory-perceptual and acoustic measures of pitch, resonance, loudness, articulation, and intonation were found to be associated with listeners' perceptions of speaker gender. Tempo and stress were not significantly associated. Mixed findings were found as to the contribution of a breathy voice quality to gender perception. Nonetheless, there exists significant risk of bias in this body of research." [JSLHR](https://pubs.asha.org/doi/10.1044/2017_JSLHR-S-17-0067)
- Hillenbrand & Clark 2009: "shifting either f 0 or formants alone was usually ineffective in changing the perceived sex." [APP](https://doi.org/10.3758/app.71.5.1150)
- Zimman 2020: speakers "may be perceived as female even with a very low mean F0 (e.g., 119–128 Hz in Günzburger 1995) or perceived as male even with a very high mean F0 (e.g., 181 Hz in Gelfer and Schofield 2000)". [Oxford Handbook chapter](http://www.lalzimman.org/PDFs/Zimman2020TransLanguageTransMoment.pdf)
- Self-rating matters more than a stranger's rating. Hancock 2011: "QoL ratings correlate more strongly with speaker’s self-rated perception of voice compared with others’ perceptions". [J Voice](<https://www.jvoice.org/article/S0892-1997(10)00136-0/abstract>)

**Avoid implying a number is the answer.** Davies 2015: "we recommend that consumers be cautious of any materials promoting a rigid set of speech norms, as speech is highly individualized and culturally driven and cannot be guided solely by a set of generic rules." Davies picks a training pitch for ease, not for a norm: one "that is either in gender neutral or low cisfemale range and can be produced without strain."

**Inference for the app:** make the target band something the user sets, label it "your range", and do not name bands "feminine" or "male". Show research bands only behind a "where do these numbers come from" note that includes the disagreement above. Avoid pass/fail colours, scores and "goal reached". These figures mostly come from English speakers; I did not look for Spanish-language data, and Davies warns that gender cues are specific "to each client’s (linguistic and cultural) community".

## 5. Spectral centroid, resonance and formants

Short answer: a weak proxy. It may stay as a rough "brightness" readout, but it should not be called resonance or be treated as "higher is better".

- Best direct evidence: Netzorg et al. 2025, one trans-feminine voice teacher, 25 configurations, ranking accuracy (Pitch / Resonance / Weight): Avg F0 99.3 / 49.6 / 43.9; ΔF (formant spacing) 61.5 / 93.8 / 55.6; Spectral Gravity 72.3 / 71.7 / 72.5; Spectral Slope 50.0 / 79.1 / 79.3. Spectral gravity is the centroid family, and it moves about equally with all three. The paper: "for intra-speaker measurements, F¯0 and ∆F can serve as proxy measurements for Pitch and Resonance." It is a conference paper with one speaker. [Interspeech](https://www.isca-archive.org/interspeech_2025/netzorg25_interspeech.pdf)
- It moves with effort. Harwardt 2011, comparing normal and raised effort for centre of gravity and first spectral moment: "Both are increased more than 10%." [Interspeech](https://www.isca-archive.org/interspeech_2011/harwardt11_interspeech.pdf) Liénard 1999: "The amplitudes in the high-frequency range increased more than those in the lower part of the spectrum".
- It may rise with strain and breathiness too. Nguyen & Madill 2023: "the most consistent acoustic correlate of strain is a decreased spectral tilt due to added energy at high frequency regions" and "Shoji et al 63 showed significant increase in high-frequency energy levels in breathy voices." **Inference:** a meter that rewards a higher centroid could reward pushing. This is the biggest design risk in the current feature set.
- Formants are the better construct, but hard to track. Anikin 2023: "Automatic formant measurement with linear predictive coding is error-prone" and advises to "record several calls or vowels per individual, measure at least three or four formants, check formant measurements manually, treat uncertain values as missing". [BRM](https://link.springer.com/article/10.3758/s13428-023-02288-x)

**Formants and perceived gender.** Sources disagree on weight, not on relevance:

- Skuk & Schweinberger 2014: "F0 and vocal tract resonances have a comparable impact on voice gender perception." [JSLHR](https://pubs.asha.org/doi/10.1044/1092-4388%282013/12-0314%29)
- Leung 2021, 379 speakers: "substantially larger contribution of F to listener perceptions of speaker gender and vocal femininity–masculinity relative to f os than has previously been reported." [JSLHR](https://pubs.asha.org/doi/10.1044/2021_JSLHR-20-00747)
- Gelfer & Bennett 2013: "Vowel formants appeared to be important to perception of gender, especially for SFFs in the range of 145-165 Hz; however, formants may be a more salient cue in connected speech when compared with isolated vowels or syllables." [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23415148/)
- Against, Gelfer & Mikos 2005, isolated vowels: "in all cases, gender identifications were based on fundamental frequency, even when fundamental frequency and formant frequency information was contradictory." [J Voice](<https://www.jvoice.org/article/S0892-1997(04)00172-9/abstract>)
- UCSF: "There is some discrepancy in the literature on the role of resonance". UCSF also writes "formant (harmonic) frequencies". Formants and harmonics are different things, so do not reuse that wording.

**Inference for the app:** label it "brightness". Compare only same vowel, similar pitch, similar level, same mic, same person, and show no gender label. If resonance matters later, consider vowel-locked formant estimates that report "no reading" when unsure.

**On "passing".** Smith 2020 (ASHA Perspectives): "these concepts and language are considered problematic because they are cisnormative and fortify the idea that only individuals consistently perceived as assigned at birth as their affirming gender are valid (GLAAD, n.d.). Nonetheless, many clients still approach therapy with the target of passing" [ASHA SIG](https://pubs.asha.org/doi/10.1044/2020_PERSP-19-00117) SOC8 does not use the word at all (0 hits in the full text). Davies 2015 does, as the person's own choice: "how important passing is to the client". **Inference:** the app should not use the word or promise the outcome, and it should not judge anyone who wants it. Safety is a real reason some people want it.

**Goals are personal.**

- ASHA: "Affirming goals focus on creating a voice that is authentic, comfortable, and safe (Hancock, 2015). Some people prefer a “gender-neutral” vocal presentation, whereas some people require flexibility for a more masculine or feminine presentation, depending on social or work environments."
- ASHA: "Goals related to voice and communication are specific to each person and may change over time." and "Do not assume the areas of communication a client will want to work on."
- UCSF: "Some transmasculine spectrum people seek only some voice masculinization, and desire flexibility with their voice and communication."
- UBC on Changing Keys: people may "realize they are happy with their voice as it is." [UBC](https://languagesciences.ubc.ca/news-events/announcement-research-news-story/jul-30-2020-finding-your-voice-gender-affirming-voice) ASHA on self-judgment: "The client may have a hypercritical self-perception and may express frustrations about their progress (Azul et al., 2018)."
- **Gap:** ASHA notes "Language is dynamic and terminology evolves." I found no style guide written for voice apps, and no source on trans-inclusive Spanish wording in voice work.

## 7. The app's exercises

"Recognised" below means used in clinical or teaching practice. No source I read calls any of these safe to do unsupervised. Titze 2006, on trills, humming and tubes: "Little has been done, however, to provide the scientific underpinnings." [JSLHR](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16671856/) Recognition does not equal trial evidence. SOC8: "Weaknesses of the identified studies include lack of randomized controlled trials evaluating voice training, small sample sizes, inadequate long-term follow-up, and lack of control of confounding variables."

## Could not source

- Limits: a session length or daily limit for people practising alone, a rest period after symptoms, a numeric loudness limit, a safe hold length for tones or "sss"/"fff".
- Evidence: any validated app or consumer mic that detects strain, injury rates from self-guided trans voice practice, anything on dizziness from breath work.
- Named sign: "throat tickle".
- Spanish: trans-inclusive wording in voice work, and pitch data for Spanish speakers (neither was searched in depth).
- Vanderbilt: only general voice care pages, no trans-specific figures.
- Raising (added 2026-11-08): how fast to raise or how big a step to take, a safe upper speaking pitch, falsetto versus the speaking voice, and a consumer-facing description of a pressed or squeezed sound. The nearest are ASHA's "hyperfunctional voicing patterns" and §3's "no standardized acoustic definition of strained voice quality". The app has no text on any of these.
